<section id="what-is-gpa">
  <h2>What is GPA?</h2>
  <p>
    Granulomatosis with Polyangiitis (GPA) is a rare autoimmune disease that causes inflammation in small and medium‑sized blood vessels. It most often affects the sinuses, lungs, and kidneys, and can show up with symptoms like chronic sinus problems, coughing, shortness of breath, fatigue, joint pain, or blood in the urine. It’s serious, but with early diagnosis and treatment many people do well and are able to keep living their lives.
  </p>
  <p>For more detailed medical explanations, I recommend these pages:</p>

<section id="patient-support">
  <h2>Patient Organizations and Support</h2>
  <p>
    The Vasculitis Foundation is the primary advocacy and support organization for people with GPA and other forms of vasculitis. They offer multiple virtual support group meetings that are free to attend with registration. The foundation hosts weekly general support meetings, evening support groups (second Tuesday of each month at 7:00 PM CT), and specialized groups including one for EGPA patients and a teen chat for young people ages 13 to 19.
  </p>
  <p>
    For individual peer connection and discussion, Mayo Clinic Connect hosts an active GPA discussion group where people share experiences and practical advice. While online communities can be valuable sources of support, approach Facebook groups and unmoderated forums with caution, as they sometimes amplify anxiety or spread misinformation. Stick with groups affiliated with established medical institutions or the Vasculitis Foundation when possible.
  </p>

<section id="living-with-gpa">
  <h2>Living with GPA</h2>
  <p>
    GPA is not just lab results and imaging reports; it’s the daily reality of fatigue, uncertainty, and figuring out what “normal” looks like now.
  </p>
  <p>
    I’m collecting gentle, practical resources about coping, pacing, and staying present while handling medical appointments, tests, and treatment side effects.
  </p>

  <h3>Patient Experiences</h3>
  <p>
    The Vasculitis Foundation shares stories that offer honest perspectives on diagnosis, treatment, and daily life with GPA. Tonya Campbell's journey, shared in 2026, describes a path from mysterious symptoms through diagnosis to managing chronic complications, including fatigue, kidney disease, and the challenge of balancing medical care with everyday life.
  </p>
  <p>
    The GPA-info.org site also features narratives about managing sinus issues, fatigue, and joint pain while trying to keep an active life through consistent treatment.
  </p>
  <p>
    Living with GPA means navigating exhaustion even with adequate sleep, managing chronic sinus problems, and accepting that remission doesn't mean the disease disappears. People often describe the importance of tracking symptoms, keeping medication journals, and building a support system that includes both medical providers and people who understand the reality of chronic illness.
  </p>

<section id="research-guidelines">
  <h2>Research and Clinical Guidelines</h2>
  <p>
    The current standard of care for GPA is guided by joint recommendations from the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) and the Vasculitis Foundation, published in 2021, and by the European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) guidelines updated in 2024. Both sets of guidelines recommend rituximab (Rituxan) as the preferred treatment for relapsing GPA and recommend reduced glucocorticoid regimens to minimize long-term harm from prolonged steroid exposure.
  </p>
  <p>
    For people interested in following current research, ClinicalTrials.gov maintains a searchable database of ongoing clinical trials for GPA. Recent studies include investigations of CAR T cell therapy for relapsing or refractory ANCA-associated vasculitis and biomarker research to improve disease monitoring.
  </p>

<section id="treatment-overview">
  <h2>Treatment Options Overview</h2>
  <p>
    This section is meant to give a high-level overview of common treatment approaches so you have some context for conversations with your care team. It is not medical advice.
  </p>
  <p>
    Rituximab (Rituxan) has become the standard treatment for GPA, particularly for relapsing disease, and is more effective than azathioprine in maintaining remission. Studies show that major relapse occurred in only 3% of rituximab patients versus 29% of azathioprine patients at 28 months. However, rituximab does carry risks including increased infection rates and infusion-related reactions, particularly during the first infusion.
  </p>
  <p>
    Maintenance therapy typically continues for 24 to 48 months following remission, with longer treatment for people at higher relapse risk. The goal of modern treatment is to achieve remission while minimizing long-term damage from both the disease and the treatments themselves, particularly glucocorticoids.
  </p>
