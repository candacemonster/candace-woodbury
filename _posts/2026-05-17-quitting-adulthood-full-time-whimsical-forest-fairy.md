I would like to formally resign from adulthood and take up my true vocation: full-time whimsical forest fairy with a garden, a stack of library books, good coffee, and too many dogs, fully funded by my husband.

If you’re curious about how I got here, I wrote more about turning forty and the version of my life that didn’t happen [over here](https://candacewoodbury.com/essays/2026/05/06/this-is-fourty/).

All I want instead is painfully simple: dogs snoring on the floor, dirt under my nails, trees outside the window, a mug in my hand, and enough calm in my nervous system to actually notice that I’m alive.

The life I have on paper is fine. Respectable, even. There’s a job with responsibilities and meetings. There are bills that get paid more or less on time, a credit score, and a calendar full of things that prove I am a functioning adult. From the outside, it passes for “having it together.” From the inside, it often feels like I’m spending all my limited energy keeping a machine running that I never really wanted to build in the first place.

Burnout is a boring word for what it feels like. “Burnout” sounds like I just need a long weekend and a scented candle. This is more like standing in a life I worked hard for and realizing I’m too tired to enjoy any of it. I’ve put my body behind glass for years in the name of work and responsibility, and now it’s banging on the window asking for trees, sun, and the kind of quiet you can’t get from a white-noise app. It’s not that I want to do nothing. I just don’t want to keep doing this.

My preferred job description these days is short and embarrassingly clear: love my husband, love my dogs, tend a garden, read books, walk in the forest, be kind to the people within reach.

That’s it. That’s the whole list. I do not secretly long to “scale” into a brand. I would like to downgrade my ambitions to “content, useful, and outside as much as possible” and call it good. Let other people chase legacy; I want to know which bird is making that sound.

In the fantasy version, my husband handles the part where money has to exist. He does the calls, the forms, the insurance, the various portals that demand sacrifices of time and patience. I grow tomatoes that may or may not cooperate, keep the dogs alive and loved, and know exactly where my library card is at all times. 

We trade the noise of “more, more, more” for something smaller, slower, and actually ours.

Reality is less romantic. There are still bills with both our names on them. There is still work, which refuses to evaporate just because my priorities have shifted. There is still a future we’re supposed to plan for, even if my preferred retirement plan is “live in a small place near trees until further notice."

So for now I haven’t burned it all down. I show up. I log in. I do what has to be done, even on the days when my whole body is screaming that this cannot be all there is.

What I can do is start tilting the balance. Take my coffee outside instead of drinking it hunched over a screen. Put my hands in actual dirt as often as my body allows. Let the dogs pull me toward the nearest patch of green and pretend that’s a non-negotiable appointment.

Say no to one more thing that looks good on paper but costs me hours I can’t get back.

Remember that wanting a small, gentle, woods-adjacent life is not a failure of imagination; it’s a perfectly reasonable response to a world that keeps insisting we should be exhausted and grateful at the same time.

I don’t know how long I have, and I don’t get the version of life where my body never complains. What I can have, if I’m brave and stubborn enough, is this: dogs, garden, forest, books, coffee, and a life that feels, if nothing else, like it at least belongs to me.
