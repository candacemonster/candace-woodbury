I’m quietly stepping back from the version of adulthood that asks for more than I can give.

On paper, my life looks fine: a job, bills paid on time, a calendar that proves I can pass for a functioning adult. From the inside, it often feels like I’m spending my limited energy keeping a machine running that I never really asked for.

What I want instead is almost embarrassingly simple: dogs snoring on the floor, dirt under my nails, trees outside the window, a warm mug in my hand, and enough calm to actually notice I’m alive.

These days my preferred job description is short: love my husband, love my dogs, tend a garden, read books, walk in the forest, be kind to the people within reach.

I’m not trying to scale into a brand. I’d rather downgrade my ambitions to “content, useful, and outside as much as possible” and call it good.

There are still bills, still work, still a future to plan for. But I’m starting to tilt the balance: coffee outside instead of hunched over a screen, hands in dirt, walks with the dogs treated like standing appointments.

Say no to one more thing that looks good on paper but costs me hours I don’t get back.

Wanting a small, gentle, woods‑adjacent life isn’t a failure of imagination; it’s a reasonable response to a world that keeps trying to convince us that exhaustion is a love language.
